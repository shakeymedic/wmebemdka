A Deep Dive into Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

Pathophysiology and Management for UK Emergency Clinicians

The Pathophysiology of DKA: A Vicious Cycle

DKA is driven by a state of absolute or relative insulin deficiency, coupled with an excess of counter-regulatory hormones (like glucagon, cortisol, and catecholamines). This hormonal imbalance triggers three core pathological processes.

1. Hyperglycaemia

Insulin deficiency impairs glucose uptake by cells and promotes uncontrolled glucose production by the liver (gluconeogenesis) and breakdown of glycogen (glycogenolysis).

↓

Osmotic Diuresis

High blood glucose overwhelms the kidneys' ability to reabsorb it, leading to glucose in the urine (glycosuria). Water follows the glucose, causing profound dehydration and loss of electrolytes (Na⁺, K⁺, PO₄³⁻).

2. Ketogenesis

The body, starved of glucose for fuel, switches to breaking down fat (lipolysis). The liver metabolises these free fatty acids into acidic ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate).

↓

Metabolic Acidosis

These ketones overwhelm the body's buffering capacity, leading to a high anion gap metabolic acidosis. The body attempts to compensate by deep, rapid breathing (Kussmaul's respiration) to blow off CO₂.

3. The Vicious Cycle

Dehydration leads to poor tissue perfusion and renal impairment, worsening the acidosis. The acidosis itself impairs insulin function and exacerbates dehydration by causing vomiting.

↓

Worsening State

This cycle of dehydration, acidosis, and electrolyte disturbance continues, leading to cardiovascular collapse, altered consciousness, and potentially death if untreated.

Diagnosis and Severity Assessment

Diagnostic Triad (UK Criteria)

  • 1.
    Hyperglycaemia: Capillary blood glucose >11.0 mmol/L or known diabetes mellitus.
  • 2.
    Ketonaemia: Capillary blood ketones >3.0 mmol/L or significant urinary ketones (++ or more).
  • 3.
    Acidosis: Bicarbonate <15.0 mmol/L and/or venous pH <7.3.

Assessing Severity

Determines patient disposition (ward vs HDU/ICU). Any ONE of the following indicates severe DKA:

  • Biochemical: Venous pH <7.1, Bicarbonate <5.0 mmol/L, Potassium <3.5 mmol/L, Anion Gap >16.
  • Clinical: GCS <12, Systolic BP <90 mmHg, Heart Rate >100 or <60 bpm, Oxygen Sats <92% on air.

Clinical Pearl: Euglycaemic DKA (eDKA)

Be aware that patients on SGLT-2 inhibitors (the "-gliflozins") can develop DKA with normal or only mildly elevated blood glucose. Any unwell patient on these drugs requires a capillary ketone measurement, regardless of their BM.

ED Management: A Step-by-Step Approach

1

Fluid Resuscitation

The first and most critical step. Start with **0.9% Sodium Chloride**. A typical adult requires 5-8 litres in the first 24 hours.

  • Initial Bolus: 1000ml 0.9% Saline over 1 hour.
  • Subsequent fluids: 1000ml over 2 hours, then 1000ml over 4 hours, then 1000ml over 6 hours. Adjust based on clinical assessment and U&Es.
  • Once blood glucose is **<14 mmol/L**, switch to **10% Dextrose** at ~125ml/hr (alongside the saline) to prevent hypoglycaemia while the insulin infusion continues to suppress ketogenesis.
2

Insulin Therapy

Start a **Fixed Rate Intravenous Insulin Infusion (FRII)** 1 hour after starting fluids. Use a weight-based calculation.

  • Dose: 0.1 units per kg per hour (e.g., 7 units/hr for a 70kg patient).
  • DO NOT give an initial IV bolus of insulin.
  • DO NOT stop the patient's long-acting background insulin (e.g., Lantus, Levemir).
3

Potassium Replacement

Patients in DKA have a total body potassium deficit, even if serum levels are initially normal or high. Insulin drives potassium into cells, causing levels to fall rapidly.

  • If K⁺ >5.5 mmol/L: Do not add potassium initially, but recheck frequently.
  • If K⁺ 3.5-5.5 mmol/L: Add 40mmol KCl to each 1000ml bag of fluid.
  • If K⁺ <3.5 mmol/L: Requires senior review and more aggressive replacement, potentially delaying insulin infusion.
4

Monitoring & Goals

Close monitoring is essential to guide therapy and avoid complications.

  • Hourly: Capillary glucose and ketones.
  • 1-2 Hourly: Venous blood gas (for pH, bicarb, K⁺).
  • 4-6 Hourly: Full U&Es.
  • Goals of Treatment:
    • Reduce ketones by ≥0.5 mmol/L/hr.
    • Increase bicarbonate by ≥3.0 mmol/L/hr.
    • Reduce capillary glucose by ~3.0 mmol/L/hr.
    • Maintain potassium in range 4.0-5.5 mmol/L.
